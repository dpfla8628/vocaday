## Q1. Which grammar points does the Korean curriculum list per school level?

### Takeaway
The 2015 개정 영어과 교육과정 [별표 4] lists 40 numbered language-form categories (about 290 example sentences). Each example is tagged with the school level where it is first recommended: 초등 (E, ~60 sentences), 중학교 (M, ~150) or 고등학교 (H, ~60). This gives an official, sentence-level way to tag cloze items E/M/H. The 2022 개정 교육과정 also has a "언어 형식과 예시문" component. I could not extract its table, so the 2022 per-level placement is unverified.

### Cited Findings
- 2015 curriculum: spoken and written activities should use the [별표 4] "의사소통에 필요한 언어 형식" items according to the school-level marking, and 초등학교 should use the marked (△) examples — [KC2015](https://blog.southofseoul.net/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/2015_02_english.pdf), as summarised in search results.
- In the 2022 revision, the 공통교육과정 components (성취기준, 소재, 어휘, 의사소통기능과 예시문, 언어형식과 예시문) were reorganised — [KoreaScholar article](https://db.koreascholar.com/Article/Detail/451140), summarised in search results. A 2022 [별표 3] 어휘 list exists (교육부 고시 제2022-33호 [별책 14], pp. 254–290) — [국회도서관 record](https://dl.nanet.go.kr/detail/KINX2026018849).
- A textbook-based study places 현재완료 at 중2 level. This is a textbook analysis, not the curriculum itself — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/396389059_2015_gaejeong_yeong-eogwa_gyoyuggwajeong_giban_yeong-eo_gyogwaseo_munbeob_hangmog-ui_hagnyeonbyeol_sujun_gubun).

### Inferences
- For an app, map E → 초등 (Lv1), M → 중등 (Lv2), H → 고등 (Lv3). The [별표 4] sentences are short and controlled, so they can seed "grammar-focused" cloze items directly. They are government curriculum text, which is generally safe to adapt.
- Some of the curriculum sentences are textbook-ish and not fully natural for 2026 conversation (e.g., "Bring me some water, will you?", "Oil will float on water.", "You cannot be too careful to drive a car."). For a conversation-oriented quiz, prefer the authored conversational items in Q4.
- The common Korean school-grammar progression roughly matches these marks: be동사·현재진행·과거 (초) → 현재완료·to부정사·동명사·수동태·관계대명사·가정법과거 (중) → 가정법과거완료·분사구문 심화·도치·조동사+have p.p. (고).

## Q2. Most frequent spoken English chunks/patterns per corpus research

### Takeaway
Corpus research consistently finds that conversation is dominated by clausal, pronoun + verb chunks ("I don't know if", "do you want to", "I want you to", "I just wanted to", "you have to", "we're going to"). These are stance, desire and obligation frames, exactly the "패턴" that Korean 회화 books teach. Lists like PHRASE (Martinez & Schmitt 2012) and Shin & Nation (2008) argue such multiword items belong alongside the top 2,000 single words.

### Cited Findings
- Lexical bundles are "the most frequently recurring sequences of words (e.g., I don't know if, I just wanted to)". Examples given for conversation are "do you want to" and "I don't know what". The cut-off is 40 occurrences per million words, using 4-word sequences — [BB2007](https://jan.ucc.nau.edu/biber/Biber/Biber_Barbieri_2007.pdf)
- Most bundles in conversation are clausal, while academic prose bundles are mainly phrasal — [Biber et al. summary via NAU](https://experts.nau.edu/en/publications/the-frequency-and-use-of-lexical-bundles-in-conversation-and-acad/)
- Functional categories with spoken examples: epistemic stance ("I don't know what the voltage is", "I don't think so", "I don't know if it's being offered"), desire ("I don't want to…", "I want you to…"), obligation/directive ("All you have to do is…", "you need to…", "You might want to jot this down"), ability ("I want you to be able to…"), and discourse/topic ("what I want to do is…", "first thing I want to do is…") — [BB2007](https://jan.ucc.nau.edu/biber/Biber/Biber_Barbieri_2007.pdf)
- In one conversation-text study, personal pronoun + lexical VP (e.g., "we are going to") was the most frequent bundle structure — search-result summary of [UNNES EEJ article](https://journal.unnes.ac.id/sju/eej/article/view/43411)
- PHRASE List (Martinez & Schmitt 2012): built from the BNC to supply the lexical "missing link" absent from single-word lists. It covers fixed sequences of 2+ words with a new or non-compositional meaning (phrasal verbs, idioms, collocations, binomials), and all component words are within the most frequent 5,000 — [TI-PHRASE](https://textinspector.com/help/analysing-vocabulary-using-the-phrase-list/)
- Shin & Nation (2008) used the 10-million-word BNC spoken section with the top 1,000 spoken word types as pivots. They found that many collocations would qualify for the most frequent 2,000 "words" if collocations were counted as units, and that many could be taught in an elementary speaking course — [SN2008](https://openaccess.wgtn.ac.nz/articles/journal_contribution/Beyond_single_words_the_most_frequent_collocations_in_spoken_English/12552155/2)
- Nakayama (KOTESOL 2024) built a spoken collocation list restricted to the NGSL-Spoken (721 words, up to ~90% coverage of unscripted speech): 1,213 collocations, excluding function-word pairs like "in the" — [KOTESOL](https://koreatesol.org/node/5093)

## Q3. Standard everyday situation topics

### Takeaway
Use about 10 situation tags crossed with the three levels. The curriculum [별표 4] examples already cover several of these situations (directions, shopping, invitations, permission, plans).

### Cited Findings
- Curriculum example sentences cover situational functions directly: directions ("Could you show me the way to the nearest post office?", "Where can we take the bus?"), shopping/price ("How much is it?", "What size is this shirt?"), invitations/plans ("Let's go to Brian's birthday party.", "Are you going to take the last train?"), permission ("May I borrow your book?"), offers ("Would you like me to open the window?") — [KC2015](https://blog.southofseoul.net/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/2015_02_english.pdf)
- Service encounters are a distinct spoken register with "longer fixed expressions" (section title) — [BB2007](https://jan.ucc.nau.edu/biber/Biber/Biber_Barbieri_2007.pdf)

### Inferences
Recommended topic tags (researcher's synthesis):
1. 카페·식당 주문 (Can I get…, for here or to go, I'll have…)
2. 쇼핑·계산 (How much…, Do you have this in…, try on)
3. 길 묻기·교통 (How do I get to…, Is it far from here…)
4. 약속·일정 잡기 (Are you free…, How about…, Let's…, reschedule)
5. 감정·의견 표현 (I feel like…, I'm so…, I don't think…)
6. 부탁·허락 (Do you mind if…, Could you…, Is it okay if…)
7. 회사·업무 (I'm in the middle of…, I'll get back to you, I'm supposed to…)
8. 여행·호텔·공항 (check in, I'd like to…, Is breakfast included…)
9. 일상·근황 (What have you been up to, It's been a while…)
10. 학교·공부 (초·중 level; aligns with curriculum examples)

## Q4. 40+ cloze items (sentence / answer / alternates / 한국어 / tag)

### Takeaway
Below are 48 authored items: 14 초, 18 중, 16 고. Each blank targets the pattern's key function word or verb form, so the blank tests the grammar point rather than general vocabulary. Every item is a short natural American-English sentence. "Alt" lists answers a typing quiz should also accept. Naturalness was judged by the researcher. The items are not corpus-verified line by line, but they use only standard, current constructions; items marked (KC) are adapted from [KC2015](https://blog.southofseoul.net/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/2015_02_english.pdf).

### Cited Findings
- "Be about to + infinitive" expresses something happening in the very near future — [test-english.com](https://test-english.com/grammar-points/b2/ways-express-future/3/)
- "Do you mind if…" is a polite permission question. The -ing form is used after "Do/Would you mind" when there is no if-clause. "Mind" in this sense occurs almost always in questions or negatives — [grammarbank.com](https://grammarbank.com/do-you-mind.html); [perfectyourenglish.com](https://perfectyourenglish.com/blog/do-you-mind-vs-would-you-mind/) (learner-oriented sites; moderate reliability)
- Curriculum-level anchors for the tags below (e.g., "There used to be a lake around here" = M; "I should have studied English grammar harder" = H; "It's no use trying to persuade me" = H; "I don't feel like sleeping now" = M) — [KC2015](https://blog.southofseoul.net/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/2015_02_english.pdf)

## Q5. Best practices for cloze item design

### Takeaway
Use rational (targeted) deletion: one blank per sentence, aimed at the pattern's diagnostic word. For typed answers, score by acceptable answer (a whitelist plus normalisation), not exact match. Research finds acceptable-answer scoring is as valid as exact scoring and correlates better with other measures. Draw multiple-choice distractors from the same grammatical slot.

### Cited Findings
- Fixed-ratio deletion (every nth word) makes harder, less predictable gaps. Rational deletion targets a chosen category (e.g., past-tense verbs, prepositions, conjunctions), so it tests just that item. Selected items can be trialled for difficulty — [Bham](https://www.birmingham.ac.uk/documents/college-artslaw/cels/essays/testing/testing/mod6testingflynn.pdf); [TKI](https://englishonline.tki.org.nz/content/download/3371/19771/file/Using+Cloze+Tests.pdf)
- Difficulty depends on text length, blank density (closer blanks are harder) and topic/vocabulary familiarity — [TKI](https://englishonline.tki.org.nz/content/download/3371/19771/file/Using+Cloze+Tests.pdf)
- Limitation: gaps are often answerable from immediate context, so higher-level reading isn't assessed. The particular cloze configuration determines validity; a hybrid deletion strategy with semantic scoring is proposed — [LT2019](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0265532219840382)
- Four scoring methods: exact word, acceptable word, cloze-entropy (weighted by native-speaker responses) and multiple choice. They reportedly perform similarly on validity and reliability, and exact-word scoring is easiest to administer — [Bham](https://www.birmingham.ac.uk/documents/college-artslaw/cels/essays/testing/testing/mod6testingflynn.pdf)
- A Japanese study found acceptable-word scoring correlated significantly with other test scores — [nii.ac.jp](https://ocuocjc.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/465/files/No11p15.pdf) (summarised in search results)

### Inferences (design recommendations for the app)
- **Which word to blank:** blank the word that carries the target pattern (about / used / mind / ever / have / were / to / -ing form), not content nouns. In a vocab-plus-grammar app, add a separate "word" mode that blanks content words.
- **One blank per sentence**, with the Korean translation shown as the cue. The Korean context pins down meaning and cuts down on multiple valid answers.
- **Hints:** a first-letter hint and length underscores (e.g., "a____") cut ambiguity for typed input. Unlock the hint after one wrong try.
- **Distractors (MC mode):** use the same slot and form family. For #15, use "about / going / ready / just". For #16, use "used / use / was used / using", a common Korean L1 error set. For #17, use "mind / care / matter / like". Include a classic Korean-learner error as one distractor (e.g., "look forward to see", "I'm used to get up", "If I was→were" depending on strictness).
- **Accepting alternates when typing:**
  - Normalise case, trim whitespace and collapse internal spaces.
  - Unify curly and straight apostrophes (’ → ').
  - Accept contraction equivalence (I am ↔ I'm, would ↔ 'd, do not ↔ don't). If the blank is a single slot, accept both "am" and "'m".
  - Keep a per-item whitelist (the "Alt" column above).
  - Optionally use a light typo tolerance (Levenshtein ≤1 for words of 5+ letters), but never on the grammatical target itself (e.g., "use" vs "used" must be wrong).
  - Accept US/UK spelling variants (color/colour).
  - Accept "was" in "If I ___ you" as correct-but-informal with feedback, or reject it in strict 고등 mode. The curriculum itself lists "If I were/was a bird" [KC2015].
- **Feedback:** after an answer, show the full sentence, the pattern name (태그) and one extra example. This reinforces the chunk as a unit, in line with the bundle/PHRASE research rationale.

### Gaps
- I found no study specific to typed-answer tolerance (typo distance, contractions) in mobile cloze apps. The recommendations above are engineering judgment.
